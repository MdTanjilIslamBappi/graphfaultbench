# GraphFaultBench: A Robustness Benchmark for Code-Graph-Guided Retrieval

**Subtitle:** Measuring localisation degradation under controlled, synthetic corruption of repository dependency graphs, and the limits of one adaptive fallback policy

**Status:** condensed from the full manuscript (`paper/manuscript.md`), revised 2026-10-21 into first-person voice, under the competition's 3,000-word limit. See the manuscript for extended methodology, tables and discussion.

## Abstract

Software agents that localise the code relevant to an issue increasingly rely on repository dependency graphs — call, import and inheritance relationships — alongside lexical retrieval. Published systems report that such graphs improve localisation accuracy, but a graph built by static analysis in a real deployment can be incomplete or stale, and I found little evidence on how localisation degrades as graph quality degrades, or whether an adaptive policy can mitigate that. I present GraphFaultBench, a benchmark harness that applies deterministic, severity-graduated corruption to statically built code graphs for real GitHub repositories, and I use it to score six retrieval policies — including two adaptive fallbacks, `DiagnosticGate` and a continuously-weighted variant, trading off a lexical-only and a graph–lexical hybrid policy using three diagnostic signals — against real Loc-Bench localisation tasks. I ran a pilot across six repositories (58 commits, 64 resolved tasks, one exclusion) and found the harness runs correctly end-to-end, but under three edge-only corruption operators, `DiagnosticGate` is numerically identical to a fixed hybrid baseline on every one of 1,620 invocations: a negative result I traced to two of its three diagnostic signals being insensitive to edge-only corruption by construction. A node-removal operator shows the gate's selection *can* respond to node-level corruption, switching on 50% of rows — but a further experiment shows no consistent accuracy benefit: at the two severities where the gate switches, the comparison's direction reverses between them, with confidence intervals too wide at either for me to call either direction confirmed. A continuously-weighted alternative to the binary switch is likewise inconclusive, scoring below it by point estimate at its one meaningfully differentiating severity. I treat the benchmark and corruption-operator harness as the contribution — reproducible, auditable and policy-independent — and report every adaptive-gate variant as negative or inconclusive rather than reframe it as a validated improvement.

## Introduction

Structural, graph-aided localisation of the code relevant to an issue is an active direction in LLM-based software agents. LocAgent represents a repository as a heterogeneous graph of files, classes, functions and their dependencies, and reports that multi-hop graph search over this representation substantially improves localisation accuracy relative to purely lexical retrieval. Systems built on similar graph representations report comparable gains: ARISE adds statement-level data-flow edges and reports an absolute improvement in SWE-bench Lite issue-resolve rate over an unmodified agent baseline, and RepoAtlas maintains an evolving, task-conditioned view of a repository's graph under a fixed context budget and reports a further resolve-rate gain over the strongest prior multimodal graph baseline.

These results share an implicit assumption: that the graph presented to the retrieval component is accurate and reasonably complete. In a real deployment this need not hold. Static analysis can fail to resolve a call target, an index can be stale, and dynamic language features can leave genuine structural relationships absent from any graph a type-unaware analyser can build. An agent that trusts a degraded graph as if it were complete risks acting confidently on misleading structural evidence — a sharper risk offline on consumer hardware, where there is no larger cloud-hosted model to fall back on when the local graph itself is degraded. My research question, registered before any pilot evidence existed:

*How does issue-to-code localisation degrade under controlled changes to the accuracy and completeness of a repository graph, and can a transparent fallback policy improve reliability with bounded computation?*

I decomposed this into three hypotheses, also registered beforehand: **H1**, graph-dependent ranking loses more accuracy than lexical retrieval as corruption increases; **H2**, an uncertainty-aware hybrid fallback reduces that decline relative to graph-only search; **H3**, the effect varies by issue category and graph topology. The evidence I gathered below is mixed-to-negative for H1 and H2, and does not yet address H3. Rather than treat this as a failure, I reframed the contribution, following a formal go/no-go review at my internal decision gate, to lead with the benchmark and corruption-operator harness as a reusable research resource for the "Best New Resource" category, reporting the fallback policies' limitations as specific, mechanistically explained findings rather than omitting or reframing them.

## Related Work

**Graph-aided localisation without a robustness framing.** LocAgent, ARISE and RepoAtlas each report that adding or better exploiting structural graph information improves localisation or issue-resolution accuracy, holding graph quality fixed. None studies the question I ask in this paper: what happens as the graph itself is deliberately, controllably degraded. Their techniques (data-flow slicing, evolving task-conditioned views) could be evaluated using my corruption operators in future work; I deliberately kept my baselines simpler, since my question concerns degradation behaviour, not absolute accuracy under healthy conditions.

**Noise and reliability benchmarks for agents.** AgentNoiseBench injects controllable noise into tool-using-agent benchmarks, split into user-side and tool-side categories, and reports consistent performance drops across architectures. This establishes noise-robustness benchmarking as an active area, but its scope is general tool use rather than code dependency graphs; I read a graph-corruption benchmark as a narrow, domain-specific instance of the same concern.

**The closest direct precedent, including a cautionary negative result.** Agent Retrieval Bench is the closest published work to my original fallback-policy idea. Its most relevant finding is that selective-abstention thresholds calibrated using counterfactual controls did not improve selective success on naturally occurring no-gold cases — a calibration gap between a threshold's designed trigger condition and the one it is actually asked to detect. This mirrors, under a different mechanism, my own finding (below) that `DiagnosticGate`'s thresholds did not transfer cleanly to the first corruption class I evaluated: independent evidence that threshold/condition mismatch is a recurring failure mode in this research area, not an idiosyncrasy of my implementation.

**Dataset.** Loc-Bench V1 (Hugging Face: `czlll/Loc-Bench_V1`), introduced alongside LocAgent, provides 560 real GitHub-issue rows with gold `edit_functions` labels across four issue categories. No licence is stated on its dataset card; I use it for internal research evaluation under academic fair use and do not redistribute its underlying problem statements or patches.

## Methods and Experiments

### Benchmark design

I implemented five deterministic, seeded corruption operators, each returning a corrupted graph and an auditable manifest of what changed: `missing_edges` (uniform random edge removal), `high_centrality_edges` (preferential removal from high-degree nodes), `stale_rewire` (redirecting edges to plausible but incorrect targets), `stale_symbol_refs` (dangling references to renamed/moved symbols), and `node_omission` (hiding nodes and their edges, modelling files a static analyser failed to index; it leaves gold labels untouched, so I detect a hidden gold answer separately from an ordinary ranking miss). Each uses a hash-derived seed, so a given (seed, severity) pair reproduces identically.

I score six retrieval policies on each corrupted graph: `bm25` (lexical-only), `graph_expansion` (pure graph-neighbourhood expansion), `fixed_hybrid` (a fixed-weight lexical–graph combination), `repomap` (a PageRank-based reimplementation of the idea behind aider's RepoMap, not the original tool), `DiagnosticGate`, and `WeightedDiagnosticGate`. The gate computes three diagnostic signals from the graph it is actually given — `node_coverage` (surviving node count over the original count), `anchor_confidence` (normalised top lexical match score), and `isolated_node_rate` (fraction of nodes with no surviving edges) — and, against three thresholds I fixed once during development and never fit on evaluation data (`coverage_floor = 0.6`, `isolated_ceiling = 0.5`, `anchor_floor = 0.1`), selects `hybrid` if the graph appears reliable, `lexical_only` otherwise, or `abstain` if lexical confidence itself is too low. `WeightedDiagnosticGate` keeps the same abstain rule but replaces the hard switch with a continuous weight, `1 − 0.5 × min(node_coverage, 1 − isolated_node_rate)`, fed into the same hybrid-ranking function — reproducing `fixed_hybrid` exactly on a healthy graph and pure `bm25` exactly on a maximally unhealthy one.

I filter Loc-Bench V1 rows to six repositories and group them by commit; for each commit, I build a code graph from a real, commit-pinned source snapshot, and resolve gold labels against it (excluding a row only on resolution failure). No retriever I test sees `hints_text` or patch content — only `problem_statement` — to prevent trivial leakage. I split tasks, before examining any threshold empirically, into a held-out development set (20%, hash split) and an evaluation set, so I could explore thresholds on the former without tuning against the latter. Metrics are `hit_at_k` (k ∈ {1,3,5}), `strict_acc_at_5` and mean reciprocal rank, with confidence intervals from a bootstrap clustered by repository, the unit of independence given correlated within-repository tasks.

### Experimental setup

I ran the pilot on six public repositories — `python/mypy`, `dask/dask`, `PrefectHQ/prefect`, `numpy/numpy`, `scipy/scipy`, `aio-libs/aiohttp` — at 58 commit-pinned snapshots, widening this from an initial three-repository run specifically to narrow the clustered-bootstrap confidence intervals below, seeds `[1,2,3]`, severities `[0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75]`. I pre-registered the full configuration and appended the three added repositories unchanged. No GPU, fine-tuning or paid API calls at any stage; I split the full pilot (8,100 scoring rows) across two CPU machines, taking 69 and 53 minutes respectively, peak memory 1.21 GiB. One task, `numpy__numpy-27598`, failed gold-label resolution and I excluded it — the only such exclusion across 65 attempts.

### Results

**Phase 2 — edge-only-corruption pilot.** The harness ran end-to-end on real data: I resolved 64 rows (one exclusion), split into 54 evaluation / 10 development tasks. Averaged across 1,620 scored rows per method: `bm25` hit@5 = 0.333, `fixed_hybrid` = 0.338, `DiagnosticGate` = 0.338 — identical to `fixed_hybrid` to three decimal places throughout. This is not approximate: the gate selected `hybrid` on 1,620/1,620 rows. I traced the mechanism directly in the recorded diagnostics: `node_coverage` and `anchor_confidence` are exactly 1.0 on every row, because none of the three edge-only operators changes the node set or node text, so neither signal can register corruption by construction; `isolated_node_rate` does rise (0.003 → 0.360 across severity) but never reaches `isolated_ceiling = 0.5`. With six clusters (doubled from three), confidence intervals narrow modestly but remain wide, and no method's interval excludes another's — a qualitative finding, not a statistically grounded ranking.

**Phase 3 — does the gate respond to the corruption it was designed for?** Wiring `node_omission` into a new experiment on the 10 development tasks (120 rows) tests whether `node_coverage` would respond if the graph actually lost nodes. It does: `node_coverage` falls to exactly 0.500 at severity 0.5 and 0.250 at 0.75, crossing `coverage_floor = 0.6`, and switches 60 of 120 rows (50%) to `lexical_only` — driven by `node_coverage`, not `isolated_node_rate` (max 0.4726, never reaching its own ceiling). This split reproduces independently (18 of 18 each way) on the three tasks I added by the widening, a genuine robustness check.

**Phase 4 — accuracy validation on the evaluation set.** Phase 3 shows the gate's policy selection responds to node-level corruption, not whether the switch improves accuracy, or whether this holds on the 54 evaluation tasks I had not yet used. I ran a further experiment, using the gate's unmodified thresholds, to score `bm25`, `fixed_hybrid` and `DiagnosticGate` against `node_omission`-corrupted graphs for all 54 evaluation tasks (1,944 rows). The pattern reproduces Phase 3's split result; the gate's policy is a clean step function — `hybrid` at severities 0.0–0.25, `lexical_only` at 0.5–0.75 (324 rows each). Because `DiagnosticGate` calls the corresponding baseline outright, its output is identical, row for row, to `fixed_hybrid` at low severities and `bm25` at high ones. `node_omission` hides the gold answer for a growing share of tasks (0%, 37.7%, 59.3%, 81.5% by severity); I report accuracy only over the gold-visible subset, stating the excluded fraction rather than dropping it. At the two severities where the gate switches:

| Severity | Gold-visible rows | `bm25` / `DiagnosticGate` hit@5 | `fixed_hybrid` hit@5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.50 | 66 | 0.364 [0.14, 0.61] | 0.394 [0.17, 0.64] |
| 0.75 | 30 | 0.433 [0.21, 0.69] | 0.400 [0.15, 0.68] |

At severity 0.50, `fixed_hybrid` — the policy the gate abandons — scores higher by point estimate than `bm25`. At severity 0.75 this reverses: `bm25`/`DiagnosticGate` scores higher. I first found this reversal on the original three-repository sample, traced to a data-quality fix (Limitations) that changed which rows remained gold-visible at severity 0.75, and it reproduces in the same direction on this widened six-repository sample. The gate's demonstrated ability to differentiate policy therefore does not translate into a *reliable* accuracy advantage either way. Confidence intervals narrow somewhat but remain wide and overlapping throughout (severity 0.75's visible sample is additionally thin at 30 rows), so I cannot call either direction a significant result.

**Phase 5 — a continuously-weighted alternative.** `WeightedDiagnosticGate`, scored identically on the same 54-task evaluation set (648 rows), produces non-degenerate weights (mean 0.50 → 0.875 across severity, zero abstains) and ties `DiagnosticGate`/`bm25` or `fixed_hybrid` row-for-row at severities 0.0, 0.5 and 0.75 despite using a different weight value. At severity 0.25 — its one meaningfully differentiating point — its hit@5 is 0.396 (n=101), below `DiagnosticGate`/`fixed_hybrid`'s 0.426 and above `bm25`'s 0.376: a non-significant point-estimate disadvantage, and the opposite of what a graceful-degradation account of the baseline would predict. I report this continuous alternative as inconclusive, not a positive result.

**Secondary evidence — a Gemma 4 downstream reranking demonstration.** As a small, separate demonstration, I used Gemma 4 E2B (4-bit GGUF, local CPU inference; full generation parameters in the full manuscript) to rerank `fixed_hybrid`'s own top-15 candidates for 15 tasks at two severities. Hit@5 was identical between Gemma's top-5 and `fixed_hybrid`'s own top-5 on all 30 combinations (0.267 and 0.200 respectively) — no differentiation, echoing the gate pattern above. Gemma also often did not return a clean five-item list (one identifier parseable in 14/30 calls), a distinct instruction-following limitation. I report this as secondary evidence from a small, single-model sample only.

### Discussion

Across four experiments, I do not find either adaptive gate's central idea — that cheap diagnostic signals can tell a system when to trust a graph — supported, for two distinct reasons. The first is a detection failure (Phase 2): two of three signals cannot register edge-only corruption because neither reads the property it perturbs, mirroring the calibration-gap finding reported independently in Agent Retrieval Bench for a different threshold. The second is more fundamental (Phases 3–5): given corruption the gate *can* detect, and a response it duly makes, that response still does not reliably improve accuracy. I had earlier offered an untested interpretation that `fixed_hybrid`'s residual graph signal degrades gracefully even below `coverage_floor`, so a continuously-weighted combination should do better than a hard switch. Phase 5 tests this directly and finds the opposite at its one informative point, so I do not find this supported either; the harness remains available to evaluate better-motivated alternatives, such as a combination learned on held-out data.

### Limitations

Six repository clusters (doubled from three) still give wide confidence intervals throughout; edge-only operators cannot exercise two of the gate's three signals by construction; my call-graph resolution is heuristic and name-based, with a substantial unresolved-call rate (roughly 9% for `python/mypy`, 43–76% for the other four repositories, and 107–130% for `scipy/scipy` — more unresolved attempts than resolved edges, reported rather than omitted); only same-snapshot Python imports are represented; queries deliberately exclude hint/patch text, making my reported hit rates a harder, leakage-free setting not comparable to prior work using such content; I found and fixed a graph-builder bug (duplicate node identifiers from `@typing.overload` stubs, 0.2–0.71% of nodes per commit) before running any repository reported here; one exception from the original three-repository regeneration — the severity-0.75 reversal in Phase 4 — reproduces on the widened sample and so is unlikely to be purely an artefact of that fix; and Loc-Bench V1's licence is unstated, so I do not redistribute its underlying content.

## Conclusion

I present GraphFaultBench, a benchmark and corruption-operator harness that applies deterministic, severity-graduated, auditable corruption to code dependency graphs and evaluates retrieval policies against real repositories and real localisation tasks. Across six repositories, the harness ran correctly and reproducibly, resolving 64 of 65 attempted tasks (one exclusion, reported rather than dropped), and I treat it as this work's primary contribution. I do not find either adaptive fallback I evaluated alongside it supported as a validated reliability mechanism: the binary `DiagnosticGate` fails to detect edge-only corruption (Phase 2); it does detect and respond to node-level corruption (Phase 3), but that response does not reliably improve retrieval accuracy on previously unseen evaluation data (Phase 4); and `WeightedDiagnosticGate`, a continuously-weighted alternative I built specifically to address this, is likewise inconclusive (Phase 5). I report each result as a specific, traceable finding rather than smooth it into a vague statement, consistent with my commitment to report negative and mixed results honestly. The harness remains available to evaluate better-motivated alternatives, such as a combination learned on held-out data. Whether this effect varies by issue category and graph topology (H3) I did not address here, and it remains open future work.
